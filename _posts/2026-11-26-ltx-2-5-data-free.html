LTX-2.5 is Lightricks' 22B model that generates video with a soundtrack from a text prompt. Its transformer alone is a 39.1 GiB file. Nunchaku Lite 0.5 ships it as a 4-bit SVDQuant checkpoint of 10.6 to 11.2 GiB, quantized from the weights alone with no calibration data: NVFP4 for Blackwell GPUs, INT4 with a Hadamard rotation for everything else.

In one paragraph. Lightricks publishes its own INT8 and NVFP4 transformers for LTX-2.5. Their INT8 stays closest to bf16. Ours are the fastest and the smallest, and at the same bit width our NVFP4 is 2.3 dB closer to bf16 than theirs. If matching bf16 output matters more to you than speed and memory, use their INT8.

Why 4 bits for LTX-2.5

LTX-2.5 distilled makes a 5-second, 768×512 clip with sound in 8 denoising steps. The pipeline around its 22B transformer is large too: a Gemma 4 12B text encoder (22.3 GiB in bf16), video and audio VAEs, a vocoder and a diffusion decoder that turns latents into frames. With every part in bf16 on one GPU, the pipeline peaks at 76.8 GB, just inside an 80 GB A100.

The transformer is the largest piece and the one that runs 8 times per clip, so it is where 4 bits pay off twice: in memory and, with 4-bit tensor-core kernels, in time per step.

The data-free recipe

The method is the one from the previous post: each linear layer's weight is split into a rank-32 low-rank branch kept in bf16, which carries the outliers that 4 bits represent poorly, and a 4-bit residual. Everything is computed from the weights alone. There is no calibration set, no captured activations and no per-model tuning: the same 1,344 linear layers of the transformer are selected automatically and quantized the same way.

What changes is the 4-bit format, which follows the GPU:

GPUformatrecipefile ↓
Blackwell (tested: RTX PRO 6000)NVFP4 weights and activations, rank 32no smoothing, no rotation11.2 GiB
other CUDA GPUs (tested: A100, L40S)INT4 weights and activations, rank 32smoothing exponent 0, Hadamard rotation on the input of all 1,344 quantized layers10.6 GiB

The rotation mixes each layer's input channels with a Hadamard transform before quantizing, which spreads outliers across channels so that the INT4 grid wastes less of its range on them. It is stored in the checkpoint and applied automatically at load time. On FLUX.2-klein, rotation improved INT4 by about 1.7 dB of per-layer output SQNR and made NVFP4 worse, which is why only the INT4 file uses it. That comparison was not repeated on LTX-2.5.

Reading the two tables together:

  • Fastest. On the RTX PRO 6000 our transformer step is 1.83× faster than bf16, 1.39× faster than Lightricks' INT8 and 1.65× faster than Lightricks' NVFP4. On the A100 our INT4 file is the only option faster than bf16 (15.3 against 18.5 s per clip); Lightricks' INT8 is slower than bf16 there.
  • Smallest. 10.6 to 11.2 GiB against 17.4 to 20.0 GiB, and 2.6 to 5.6 GB lower peak VRAM than Lightricks' files.
  • Better at 4 bits. At the same NVFP4 format, ours is 2.3 dB closer to bf16 than Lightricks' (21.12 against 18.86 dB). The bf16 low-rank branch is the difference: it keeps the weight outliers out of the 4-bit grid.
  • Lightricks' INT8 is closest to bf16, by about 5 dB on Blackwell (26.09 against 21.12) and 4.5 dB on the A100 (21.67 against 17.15). With twice the bits per weight, that is expected.

A lower PSNR means the video differs more from the bf16 video for the same seed. It does not have to mean the video looks worse: a distilled 8-step model turns small numerical differences into a slightly different pose or camera path, and PSNR punishes that as much as it punishes blur. Watch the comparisons below: five of the six prompts, on both GPUs.

One more caveat on precision: our quantized path is not bit-reproducible from run to run. Mean PSNR moves by about 0.3 dB between runs, and one A100 rerun of the same INT4 file scored 22.4 dB against the first run. Treat differences of a few tenths of a dB as noise.

A faster diffusion decoder

LTX-2.5 does not decode latents with a plain VAE: its decoder is itself a small diffusion model, and its last stage (stage 5) dominates decode time. Nunchaku Lite now ships three fused CUDA ops for that stage, in nunchaku-lite-kernels v3:

  • per-head q/k RMSNorm and 3D RoPE in one kernel,
  • residual add, RMSNorm and modulation in one kernel,
  • SiLU gating.

Attention stays on NATTEN: its default tiles were the fastest we tried, and there was no faster attention backend for the RTX PRO 6000's architecture. The decoder kernels work with any transformer; the bf16 numbers below use them too.

decode of 768×512×121, RTX PRO 6000time ↓
Diffusers default (tiled)3.24 s

The fused decoder matches the unfused one to 56.6 dB PSNR (largest pixel difference 7/255), the difference that reordering floating-point reductions leaves. End to end, a full NVFP4 clip goes from 8.0 to 6.2 s (55.2 GB peak) and a bf16 clip from 10.9 to 9.4 s.

Lower VRAM: the text encoder

Once the transformer is 11 GiB, the largest component left is the Gemma 4 12B text encoder at 22.3 GiB. Nunchaku Lite 0.5.1 adds load_text_encoder_bnb, which loads it with bitsandbytes 8-bit or NF4 weights straight onto the GPU. RTX PRO 6000, NVFP4 transformer, fused decoder, everything on the GPU:

text encodersize ↓hidden-state error vs bf16 ↓peak VRAM ↓s/clip ↓
bf1622.3 GiB—55.2 GB6.2
bitsandbytes 8-bit12.1 GiB1.1%46.4 GB6.4
bitsandbytes NF47.0 GiB2.9%41.9 GB7.0

With NF4 the whole pipeline peaks at 41.9 GB, down from 76.8 GB for the all-bf16 pipeline. Clip-level PSNR cannot rank the three encoders: the spread between prompts (2 to 5 dB) is larger than the differences between them, so compare a few videos for your own prompts. We also built a data-free W4A16 encoder of our own (7.7 GiB, 3.2% error). It did no better than NF4, so 0.5.1 removed it in favour of bitsandbytes.

In Lightricks' own code, and a flicker hunt

Diffusers is one way to run LTX-2.5; Lightricks' own ltx-core / ltx-pipelines is the other. Support for it is in progress in nunchaku-lite PR #23, open and ready for review. It splices our quantized layers into Lightricks' own bf16 transformer file, leaving every other tensor byte-identical, and loads the result through an ltx-core QuantizationPolicy, the same mechanism as Lightricks' nvfp4-prequant. Lightricks' loader, its key checks, streaming and offloading then work as they do for any checkpoint.

This pipeline tells a different story from Diffusers. Its two-stage pipeline uses an ancestral sampler, which amplifies any numerical difference, so absolute PSNR is lower for every file. Here Lightricks' NVFP4 is slightly ahead of ours on PSNR (by 0.34 dB, close to our run-to-run noise), SSIM and transformer time, and clearly ahead on flicker. Ours still runs the transformer about 1.5× faster than bf16 on both GPUs, and the INT4 file covers GPUs where NVFP4 does not run.

The flicker gap was the interesting part. Lightricks' NVFP4 file keeps the last 6 transformer blocks (42 to 47) in bf16; ours quantizes all 48. Splicing our layers into blocks 0 to 41 only, and keeping 42 to 47 in bf16 as Lightricks does, closes about 70% of the gap. On an RTX PRO 4500 Blackwell, over 10 clips:

Nunchaku Lite NVFP4 r32mean flicker ↓gap to Lightricks NVFP4 ↓steadier than all 48
all 48 blocks quantized (published)8.98+1.36—
blocks 42–47 kept bf168.04+0.429 of 10 clips
Lightricks NVFP4 (reference)7.62——

Two other suspects were ruled out. Restricting which layer types are quantized, in all 48 blocks, did not change flicker. Nor did the activation scales: we quantize activations with scales computed on the fly, Lightricks with fixed scales stored in the file, but emulating both on the CPU gave the same quantization error and the same frame-to-frame noise.

So why do the published files still quantize all 48 blocks? Because side by side the difference is hard to see. Play the clips below at 0.25× and judge for yourself; the difference maps (frame-to-frame change, amplified) make it easier to spot. The files stay smaller with all 48 blocks quantized, and PR #23's splice tool takes a --targets-regex option for anyone who prefers the steadier variant.

Try it

Nunchaku Lite 0.5.1 is on PyPI (release notes: 0.5.0, 0.5.1). LTX-2.5 needs Diffusers 0.43 or newer, and the decoder speed-up needs the v3 kernels, which download from the Hugging Face Hub on first use.

pip install nunchaku-lite                # CUDA kernels download from the Hugging Face Hub
pip install "nunchaku-lite[bnb]"         # optional: 8-bit / NF4 text encoder

The two checkpoints are in rootonchair/nunchaku-lite-ltx-2.5-distilled: svdq-fp4_r32-ltx-2.5-distilled.safetensors for Blackwell and svdq-int4_r32-ltx-2.5-distilled.safetensors for other CUDA GPUs. They are derivatives of LTX-2.5 under Lightricks' LTX-2.x Community License, which has its own terms, including for commercial use and for labeling generated content.

Convert the base model once

The repo holds only the transformer. The text encoder, VAEs, vocoder and diffusion decoder come from the base model in the Diffusers folder layout, and Lightricks ships LTX-2.5 as single files. Accept the license of the gated Lightricks/LTX-2.5 repo, log in, and convert with the script in the Nunchaku Lite repository. It downloads 72 GB.

hf auth login
python tools/convert_ltx25_distilled.py --out ltx-2.5-distilled-diffusers

Generate

Text to video with audio, 768×512, 121 frames, 8 steps. Set precision to "fp4" on Blackwell and "int4" elsewhere.

import json
import os

import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForImageTextToText, AutoTokenizer

import diffusers
import diffusers.pipelines.ltx2 as ltx2
from diffusers import FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler, LTX2Guidance, LTX2VideoTransformer3DModel, LTX25AutoBlocks
from diffusers.pipelines.ltx2.utils import DISTILLED_SIGMA_VALUES
from diffusers.utils import encode_video

from nunchaku_lite import patch_transformer
from nunchaku_lite.adapters.ltx2_decoder import optimize_ltx2_diffusion_decoder

model_dir = "ltx-2.5-distilled-diffusers"  # from tools/convert_ltx25_distilled.py
precision = "fp4"  # "fp4" on Blackwell, "int4" elsewhere
checkpoint = f"rootonchair/nunchaku-lite-ltx-2.5-distilled/svdq-{precision}_r32-ltx-2.5-distilled.safetensors"
device = "cuda"

# 1. quantized transformer
transformer = LTX2VideoTransformer3DModel.from_pretrained(
    model_dir, subfolder="transformer", torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16
)
patch_transformer(transformer, checkpoint, target="ltx2", precision=precision, device=device)


# 2. the other components, bf16
def load_component(name):
    path = os.path.join(model_dir, name)
    class_name = json.load(open(os.path.join(path, "config.json")))["_class_name"]
    cls = getattr(diffusers, class_name, None) or getattr(ltx2, class_name)
    return cls.from_pretrained(path, torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16)


guidance = dict(guidance_scale=1.0, stg_scale=0.0, modality_scale=1.0, guidance_rescale=0.0,
                spatio_temporal_guidance_blocks=None)  # distilled model: no guidance
components = {
    "transformer": transformer,
    "text_encoder": AutoModelForImageTextToText.from_pretrained(
        os.path.join(model_dir, "text_encoder"), torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16
    ),
    "tokenizer": AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(os.path.join(model_dir, "tokenizer")),
    "scheduler": FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler.from_pretrained(os.path.join(model_dir, "scheduler")),
    "guider": LTX2Guidance(**guidance),
    "audio_guider": LTX2Guidance(**guidance),
    **{name: load_component(name) for name in
       ("connectors", "vae", "audio_vae", "vocoder", "diffusion_decoder", "duration_head")},
}

# 3. pipeline, all on the GPU
pipe = LTX25AutoBlocks().init_pipeline()
pipe.update_components(**components)
for component in pipe.components.values():
    if isinstance(component, torch.nn.Module):
        component.to(device)

# 4. faster diffusion decoder (needs the v3 kernels)
optimize_ltx2_diffusion_decoder(pipe.diffusion_decoder)
pipe.diffusion_decoder.disable_tiling()

# 5. generate
steps = 8
state = pipe(
    prompt="A golden retriever runs through a sunlit meadow of tall grass, ears flapping, birds chirping.",
    width=768,
    height=512,
    num_frames=121,
    frame_rate=24.0,
    num_inference_steps=steps,
    sigmas=DISTILLED_SIGMA_VALUES[:steps],
    use_cross_timestep=True,
    enable_prompt_enhancement=False,
    generator=torch.Generator(device).manual_seed(0),
    output_type="np",
)
video, audio = state.get("videos"), state.get("audio")
encode_video(
    video[0],
    fps=24.0,
    audio=audio[0].float().cpu(),
    audio_sample_rate=pipe.vocoder.config.output_sampling_rate,
    output_path="ltx25_nunchaku_lite.mp4",
)

The script ran as written on an RTX PRO 6000 with both files, peaking at 55.8 GiB of VRAM with NVFP4 and 55.3 GiB with INT4. These are its two outputs:

To fit a smaller GPU, swap in the bitsandbytes text encoder: replace the "text_encoder" entry, and skip it when moving components to the GPU, since it is loaded there already.

from nunchaku_lite.text_encoders import load_text_encoder_bnb

components["text_encoder"] = load_text_encoder_bnb(
    AutoModelForImageTextToText, os.path.join(model_dir, "text_encoder"), quantization="nf4", device=device
)  # or quantization="int8"

# step 3: move everything else
for name, component in pipe.components.items():
    if isinstance(component, torch.nn.Module) and name != "text_encoder":
        component.to(device)

Limitations

  • LTX-2.5 support is a preview. The checkpoints cover the distilled model only.
  • NVFP4 runs only on Blackwell GPUs. On other CUDA GPUs, use the INT4 file.
  • Loading the transformer reads the bf16 weights first (about 44 GB of CPU memory) before replacing them with the quantized ones, and converting the base model needs about 85 GB of CPU memory.
  • Untiled decoding is faster but needs about 13.5 GB more VRAM at 768×512×121. Tiled and untiled decodes differ from each other (about 30 dB PSNR), so compare outputs only between runs that decode the same way.
  • The quantized path is not bit-reproducible from run to run (about 0.3 dB of mean PSNR).
  • Running in Lightricks' ltx-core is not released yet (PR #23). There, our NVFP4 flickers more than Lightricks' NVFP4, as described above.
